Supplement Interactions: What Happens When Products Combine

Most supplement buyers take more than one product — a multivitamin, a fish oil, a botanical or two — and few stop to ask how those products interact with each other, or with their medicines. Interactions are the supplement world's least discussed risk, and they deserve more attention than they get.

The first kind of interaction is between supplements themselves. Ingredients can compete for absorption: calcium, for example, can reduce the absorption of iron when taken at the same time. Others can add up: several products containing the same ingredient can push your total intake well above the intended dose, even when each product alone is within its label amount. And some combinations simply duplicate each other — two products with the same botanical, taken together, are not twice as effective; they are twice the dose, with twice the chance of side effects.

The second kind of interaction is between supplements and medicines, and it is the more serious one. Some botanicals affect the liver enzymes that metabolise common medicines, changing how quickly a drug is cleared from the body. Others affect blood clotting, blood pressure or blood sugar, and can amplify or blunt a medicine's effect. These interactions are well documented for some ingredients and poorly studied for others — which is itself a reason for caution, since the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The third kind of interaction is with laboratory tests. High-dose biotin is the best-known example: it can interfere with several common blood tests, including some thyroid and cardiac markers, producing misleading results. Other supplements can affect test outcomes in subtler ways. If you are having blood work done, tell the clinician and the laboratory what you take — and ask whether you should pause anything beforehand.

So what should you do? Keep a complete list of everything you take — supplements and medicines together — with doses and timing. Share it with your doctor and your pharmacist, and ask specifically about interactions, not just about each product in isolation. Space out products that compete for absorption where a clinician advises it. And when you add a new product, add it alone, so that any change you notice can be traced to the new addition rather than to a combination.
